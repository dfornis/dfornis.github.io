A companion to the research article The politics of baselines: A political feasibility framework for energy and climate scenarios · Fornborg (2026), Energy Research & Social Science

Energy and climate scenarios often rely on assumptions about future policy developments that are highly contested. Despite this, they are rarely screened for political feasibility before they enter models. This page demonstrates how a set of plausible policy responses to the rapid growth of datacenter electricity demand in Sweden can be identified, screened for political feasibility and carried into a power system model.

The case

01 The policy question

Which rules for new Swedish datacenters could be adopted and sustained as electricity demand grows?

Sweden · Policy decisions in 2026–2027 · Rules from 2028 · Follow to 2035

Why the rules are contested

  • Announced datacenter projects would add about 36 TWh to Swedish electricity use by 2035, roughly a quarter of today’s use.
  • Generation and grid capacity expand slowly: onshore wind depends on municipal approval, and offshore wind and new nuclear take years to permit and build.
  • How the cost is shared between developers, households, industry and taxpayers is a political question.

03 Define comparable trajectories

The survey points to voluntary commitments, generation requirements and restrictions on new sites. These four alternatives apply those policy directions to new commercial datacenters in Sweden from 2028. Each is expressed in modelling terms, including what counts as new generation.

Finnish consultation · Spanish draft

T2 · Moratorium scenario

Halt new establishments

No new commercial datacenter sites open.

Irish moratorium campaigns · Québec

The difference between T1 and T1b is one of specification: whether new generation must also be additional.

What the generation requirement covers

From the first full operating year, new fossil-free generation in Sweden must match 100% of each covered datacenter’s annual electricity demand. Developers invest directly or sign a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA). The generating plant must enter service after the investment or contract commitment and by the datacenter’s opening. The reform includes annual compliance reporting to the Energy Markets Inspectorate (Ei), with powers granted by the new legislation. Grid charges and connection priority remain as in T0. Under T1b, only generation that would not otherwise be built counts, and Ei verifies this.

The same prospective projects are assessed in all four alternatives: new commercial datacenter sites in Sweden that would open from 2028 or have no announced opening date. Earlier stages, existing sites and their expansions fall outside this example.

Assess decisions in 2026–2027 and whether the resulting policy can be sustained to 2035. The dates are scenario choices. Political support can change within this period.

Institutional constraintsWhich rules and decision procedures matter?

Trace the route from a political demand to a rule that authorities can apply. Distinguish formal legal constraints from political habits.

T0Voluntary commitments

Voluntary generation contracts fit the existing electricity-market framework. Continuing this arrangement requires no new sector legislation or compliance authority. Grid operators and the regulator retain their existing connection and supervision roles.

A bill with an established legal basis, assigned supervision and a timetable would remove much of this disadvantage.

T2Establishment stop

Sweden protects freedom of trade and requires impartial administration. A national moratorium would need legislation justified by an important public interest, with rules for pending projects. Politicians cannot direct the outcome of individual administrative cases covered by constitutional independence. This makes T2 a substantial legislative change; protecting selected competitors alone is not a sufficient basis.

A government bill would establish the proposed legal basis, transition arrangements and implementing authority. For T2, the justification must address why conditions on development would be insufficient.

Landscape pressuresHow could wider trends change political priorities?

Landscape pressures concern macro-level change and public opinion: the AI investment race, industrial decarbonisation, energy-price shocks and changing views of who should benefit from scarce electricity. Assess how these developments could strengthen or weaken each trajectory over time.

T0Voluntary commitments

The AI investment race and digital sovereignty favour attracting new sites. But continued demand growth without sufficient generation brings a competing priority: affordable electricity for homes and industrial electrification. Svenska kraftnät describes this price and competitiveness risk. If it persists, the political appeal of accommodating every new project weakens.

Follow changes in electricity affordability, industrial project delays, energy security and public attitudes to datacenter growth. New generation that keeps pace with demand would weaken the case for tighter restrictions.

Adjustment burdenWho loses, and can they adjust or be compensated?

Assess losses under continuation as well as reform. Include the capacity to absorb costs, secure compensation or turn those costs into political opposition.

T0Voluntary commitments

If new demand outpaces supply, households and industrial users face higher electricity bills. Firms can also lose years of investment returns while waiting for network capacity. Households can cut other spending or electricity use; firms can hedge, delay or relocate investment. SKGS represents industrial users in energy policy. T1Generation requirement

New datacenter developers must finance generation or commit to long-term payments under a PPA. The contract option avoids having to build and operate a power plant themselves. However, T1 requires the new plant to be ready when the datacenter opens: a delayed generation project can therefore delay the datacenter. Developers can respond by seeking exemptions, postponement or a different location. SweDCI’s demands for timetable guarantees show why this matters politically.

A phase-in or protection against generation-project delays would reduce developers’ resistance. Grid reinforcement charges require a separate decision: neither trajectory changes who pays them.

Policy momentumWhat do earlier policies and investments set in motion?

Previous policies establish routines, investments and expectations. Their effects can also create new interests that defend the policy or seek to reverse it.

T0Voluntary commitments

Contracting and established connection procedures make continuation easier initially. But momentum can turn: Sweden withdrew the datacenter electricity-tax concession in 2023. If further expansion raises bills or obstructs industrial investment, defending the existing policy becomes more costly and earlier supporters may seek reform.

Quantification, 2035

Each trajectory is then quantified in a Nordic power system model for 2035. Without a requirement, other Swedish customers pay about 4.5 billion euros a year more than without the new datacenters. A requirement that counts any new fossil-free generation changes little, about 4.1 billion, since it is mostly met by wind farms that would be built anyway. A requirement for additional generation reduces the extra cost to about 1.8 billion and raises the datacenters’ own electricity cost by about a tenth. A halt to new establishments gives about the same relief, 1.9 billion, as datacenters in Norway and Finland still raise Nordic prices.

Is new generation additional?

Onshore wind is built up to its permitting limit whether or not the datacenters arrive. Under T1, developers can therefore meet the requirement by contracting wind farms that would be built anyway. Only T1b, which counts generation the market would not build, lowers prices markedly. Before quantification, the specification of a trajectory therefore matters as much as its political feasibility.

Under T1b, about 60% of the added generation displaces wind and solar that would otherwise be built in Finland and Norway in the wider Nordic system; the rest reduces imports.

Matched hour by hourHow much of datacenter use each portfolio covers in the same hour

Matched hour by hour, a wind portfolio covers about 70% of datacenter use, and doubling it reaches about 87%. New nuclear covers almost all of it. An hourly requirement would favour generation that does not depend on the weather, such as nuclear power, and would change the coalitions around a generation requirement.

Modelled prices and cost shares show who gains or loses under each policy. They become evidence on adjustment burden and coalitions at the next decision window. Competition for grid connections lies outside the model and remains a qualitative assessment.

Model and assumptions

The model is PyPSA-Eur adapted for the Nordics. Wind, solar and batteries expand at least cost within published limits in every Nordic country; dispatch is then solved hour by hour, month by month, with water values for hydro storage. The model is currently in development and results are work in progress.
